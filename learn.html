The paper, section by section

    Section 1

    Introduction: the problem and the three-stage pipeline

    Paper p. 3

    A defender has a fleet of aircraft and must patrol a set of barriers (borders, choke points, sensitive areas). An intelligent attacker wants to cross or strike one of them. The paper asks: how should the defender schedule patrols so that even the smartest attacker gets as little as possible?

    The big idea The attacker can learn your policy (the long-run odds of each patrol plan) by watching for a while, but cannot see today's plan. So the defender should commit to a randomized policy that leaves no barrier noticeably weaker than the others. That is a Stackelberg security game: the defender leads (commits first), the attacker follows (best-responds).

    Three assumptions about the attacker

    1. The attacker is intelligent: they pick whatever is best for them.
    2. The attacker observes the policy (the probabilities) but not today's specific schedule.
    3. The attacker strikes the most vulnerable target, i.e. the one worst for the defender.

    The pipeline

    Everything in the paper serves one of these boxes. Click one to see what it takes in and what it produces.

    The solution pipeline

    The order matters. Stage 1 works with smooth, continuous numbers (hours can be 482.06), which makes it easy to solve exactly. Real patrols come in whole sorties, so Stage 2 turns the smooth answer into a lottery over whole-number plans. Stage 3 then makes each plan flyable: actual clock times and tail numbers.

    Section 2

    Problem parameters

    Paper p. 3, Table 1 and Remark 1

    The model has a handful of inputs you choose and a chain of derived quantities that follow from them. Getting comfortable with this chain makes every later formula easy to read.

    The big idea A sortie burns only τp flying hours but protects for W = τp + τs hours, because a barrier that was just checked stays safe for a while. So each flying hour is “worth” μ = W/τp protection hours. That multiplier μ shows up everywhere.

      Remark 1: why Beff and not B?

      The fleet can fly B = N·F hours, but sorties come in chunks of τp. The largest whole number of sorties is Nsrt = ⌊B/τp⌋, which uses Beff = Nsrt·τp hours. The leftover B − Beff is less than one sortie. Stage 1 is solved with Beff so that Stage 2 can match it exactly with whole sorties (zero residual gap). With your numbers, the leftover is .

      Section 3

      The coverage model

      Paper p. 4, Definition 1 and Remark 2

      Let hk be the total flying hours per period T spent on all barriers of type k together. How often is one particular barrier of that type protected?

      \[ P_k \;=\; \frac{\mu\,h_k}{n_k\,T} \qquad\text{(Eq. 1)} \]

      Read it as a ratio of hours: the numerator μhk is the protection-hours your flying creates; the denominator nkT is the barrier-hours that exist (nk barriers, each needing T hours). Pk is the fraction of time a given barrier is covered, which is also the probability that a randomly-timed attack finds it covered.

      The big idea Coverage is linear in hours: double the hours, double the coverage (until it hits 100%). Full coverage (Pk = 1) needs hk = nkT/μ hours (Remark 2). The formula quietly assumes the sorties are spread evenly in time; Stage 3 is what makes that true.

      Each block is one sortie: the dark part is the flight (τp), the light part is the safe period (τs). The strips show the first week of the period for each barrier of the chosen type.

      Section 4

      The Stackelberg security game

      Paper pp. 4–5: players, payoffs, equilibrium, Remark 3

      4.1 Players and actions

      • A pure strategy is one whole-number sortie plan (m1, …, mK) with Σ mk = Nsrt. It spends ak = mkτp hours on type k.
      • A mixed strategy s is a probability distribution over pure strategies. Its expected hours are hk = Σj Ajk sj.
      • The defender commits to s. The attacker sees s and picks one barrier type to hit.

      4.2 Payoffs

      If the attacker hits a type-k barrier, the defender gains vk if it was covered (probability Pk) and loses vk if it wasn't. It is zero-sum: the attacker gets the negative.

      \[ U_d(k) = v_k P_k - v_k(1-P_k) = v_k(2P_k - 1) = v_k\!\left(\frac{2\mu h_k}{n_k T} - 1\right) \qquad\text{(Eq. 2)} \]
      How payoff responds to coverage

      Every line crosses zero at P = 50%: below half coverage the defender expects to lose. Higher-value types have steeper lines, so they swing harder both ways.

      4.3 The equilibrium: max over you, min over them

      \[ \max_{h \ge 0,\ \sum_k h_k = B_\text{eff}} \;\; \min_{k} \; U_d(k; h) \qquad\text{(Eq. 3)} \]

      The inner min is the attacker picking your weakest type. The outer max is you choosing the allocation whose weakest point is as strong as possible. Try it yourself:

      Split the budget Beff = h across the three types (Low gets whatever is left). The attacker always hits the type with the lowest bar. Can you match the optimum?

      Remark 3: why the attacker ends up indifferent

      Suppose at your chosen allocation type k′ gives you a higher payoff than type k″. The attacker hits k″. But then the hours on k′ are partly wasted: move a few of them to k″, and k″ gets stronger while the attacker still has nothing better. Your worst case went up, so you weren't optimal. Repeat until no type is weaker than another. At the optimum, every type that gets any hours (the active set) gives you exactly the same payoff z*, the game value. The “Remark 3 step” button above does exactly this move; press it a few times.

      Section 5 · Stage 1

      Stage 1: the linear programme and its closed-form answer

      Paper pp. 5–6: LP (4), Theorem 1, Remark 4, Proposition 2, Remark 5

      5.1 Turning max–min into an LP

      A “max of a min” isn't linear, but a standard trick makes it so: introduce one number z that must sit below every type's payoff, then push z as high as possible.

      \[ \begin{aligned} \max_{h,\,z}\quad & z \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & z \le v_k\!\left(\tfrac{2\mu h_k}{n_k T}-1\right) \;\;\forall k && \text{(C1)}\\ & \textstyle\sum_k h_k = B_\text{eff} && \text{(C2)}\\ & h_k \ge 0 && \text{(C3)} \end{aligned} \]

      At the optimum z equals the smallest payoff, which is exactly the attacker's choice. The LP has K+1 variables and is always feasible and bounded.

      5.2 Theorem 1: you don't even need an LP solver

      By Remark 3 all (C1) constraints bind at the optimum: each payoff equals z*. Solve each for hk, add them up, and set the sum equal to the budget:

      \[ d_k = \frac{n_k T}{2\mu v_k}, \qquad z^* = \frac{B_\text{eff} - \dfrac{T N_b}{2\mu}}{\sum_k d_k}, \qquad h_k^* = d_k\,(z^* + v_k) \]

      dk is a sensitivity: how many flying hours it costs to raise type k's payoff by one unit. Many barriers or low value means large dk (expensive to move).

      Compute Stage 1 step by step

      Uses the running example from Section 2. Try predicting each number before you reveal it.

        Remark 4 (sign of z*). The numerator Beff − TNb/(2μ) compares your budget with the hours needed to hold every barrier at exactly 50% coverage. More than that and the defender expects a net gain (z* > 0); less, and even optimal patrolling loses on average.

        5.3 Active and inactive types

        h*k = dk(z* + vk) is negative if z* < −vk. Negative hours are impossible, so such a type gets zero hours and is dropped. The intuition: when you are already losing |z*| ≥ vk everywhere else, leaving type k completely open (a loss of exactly vk) is no worse. The paper lists three cases:

        • Case 1, z* > 0: every type is active.
        • Case 2, −vk < z* ≤ 0: you are losing, but type k is still worth defending.
        • Case 3, z* ≤ −vk: type k is indefensible: drop it and re-solve over the rest (Remark 5).
        The water-level picture

        Pick a target payoff level z. To lift type k up to z you need dk·(z + vk) hours, or zero if z ≤ −vk. The rising line is the total hours needed; z* is where it meets your budget. Each kink is a type switching on. Drag the fleet size and watch types become active.

        Section 6 · Stage 2

        Stage 2: from smooth hours to a lottery over whole-sortie plans

        Paper pp. 7–9: feasibility LP (9), column generation, Section 6.4, Proposition 3

        6.1 The integer problem

        Stage 1 says “spend 482.06 hours on High.” That is m*H = h*/τp = 160.69 sorties, and you cannot fly 0.69 of a sortie. No single whole-number plan hits h* exactly. But a mixture can: fly 161 some months and 160 in others, in the right proportions, and the average is exactly 160.69.

        6.2 The feasibility LP

        Collect candidate plans as rows of a matrix A (Ajk = hours plan j puts on type k). We need probabilities s with

        \[ A^\top s = h^*, \qquad \mathbf 1^\top s = 1, \qquad s \ge 0. \qquad\text{(Eq. 8)} \]

        To give a solver something to minimise, add a slack εk ≥ 0 per type (the coverage shortfall) and minimise Σεk. An optimum of 0 means h* is reproduced exactly.

        \[ \min_{s,\varepsilon}\ \mathbf 1^\top\varepsilon \quad\text{s.t.}\quad A^\top s + \varepsilon = h^*,\;\; \mathbf 1^\top s = 1,\;\; s,\varepsilon \ge 0 \qquad\text{(Eq. 9)} \]
        Build the floor/ceiling candidates (Section 6.4)

        Round each m*k down. Because Σ m*k = Nsrt exactly, the fractional parts add up to a whole number r, so exactly r types must be rounded up instead. Every way of choosing those r types is a candidate.

        Draw this month's plan

        This is the operational meaning of the mixed strategy: at the start of each period, spin a weighted wheel and fly the plan it lands on. The attacker knows the wheel, not the outcome.

        Spin to pick a plan.

        Proposition 3: you never need more than K+1 plans

        The LP (9) has K+1 equality constraints (K coverage rows plus the “probabilities sum to 1” row). A simplex solver returns a basic solution, which has at most as many non-zero variables as equality constraints. So at most K+1 plans get positive probability, no matter how many candidates you offer. With K = 3 that is at most 4; above, you see .

        6.3 Column generation (the general method)

        In general the number of whole-number plans is astronomically large, so the paper never lists them all. Column generation starts with a few plans and adds one improving plan per round.

        Duality background: reduced costs and what λk means

        Every LP min c⊤x s.t. Ax = b, x ≥ 0 has a dual with one variable per constraint. The reduced cost of a column is c̄j = cj − aj⊤y. A column not yet in the LP can lower the objective only if c̄j < 0.

        For LP (9), give each coverage row a dual λk and the probability row a dual ν. A plan costs 0 in the objective, so its reduced cost is −(aj⊤λ + ν). A new plan helps if aj⊤λ + ν > 0.

        λk is an “under-coverage signal”: how much the total shortfall drops per extra hour on type k. A large λk means type k most urgently needs hours.

        The pricing problem and the loop

        Find the plan with the largest τp Σ λkmk (Eq. 11). Every sortie is worth τpλk on type k, so the answer is simply: put all sorties on the type with the largest λk. The loop:

        1. Start with K canonical plans (plan k = all Nsrt sorties on type k).
        2. Solve LP (9) over the current plans and get λ, ν.
        3. If the gap Σε = 0, stop.
        4. Otherwise solve the pricing problem, add that plan, and repeat.

        It needs at most K+1 rounds. For K ≤ 4 the paper skips the loop and directly enumerates the floor/ceiling candidates, as in the widget above.

        Look closer · not in the paper
        The K canonical plans already reproduce h* exactly: mix them with probabilities sk = h*k/Beff (). The gap is zero on round one. So zero gap alone does not single out a good mixture: any mix with the right average passes. The canonical mix is terrible in practice, because each month it leaves two of the three types completely unpatrolled. Section 11 makes this precise and proves the floor/ceiling mix is the right choice.
        Section 7 · Stage 3

        Stage 3: turning a plan into a timetable

        Paper pp. 9–10: Eqs. 12–15, Remarks 6–9, aircraft assignment

        7.1 Spread the sorties over individual barriers

        A plan says “mk sorties on type k.” Split them as evenly as possible over the nk barriers: each gets ⌊mk/nk⌋, and the first (mk mod nk) get one extra. A barrier with qi sorties is visited every Δi = T/qi hours, and its coverage is pi = W/Δi = Wqi/T (Eqs. 12–13).

        One plan, ten barriers

        7.2 Randomised phases

        If barrier i were always patrolled at the same clock times, an attacker could watch for two days, learn the clock, and strike just after a patrol ends. So each barrier draws its own random start offset:

        \[ \varphi_i \sim \text{Uniform}[0,\;\Delta_i - W), \qquad t_{i,j} = \varphi_i + j\,\Delta_i \quad (j = 0,1,2,\dots) \qquad\text{(Eqs. 14–15)} \]
        • Remark 6: the upper limit Δi − W keeps each window inside its own cycle, so exactly W of every Δi hours are covered.
        • Remark 7: coverage pi = W/Δi doesn't depend on φi, so randomising costs nothing.
        • Remark 8: independent draws per barrier beat one shared draw per type: seeing one barrier reveals nothing about the others.
        • Remark 9: two layers of randomness. Stage 2 hides which plan is active; Stage 3 hides when each sortie flies.
        Redraw the phases

        First 72 hours of the chosen plan. Redraw and the blocks move, but each barrier's coverage number on the right never changes (Remark 7).

        Look closer: when should a clever attacker strike?

        Coverage pi is an average over time. An attacker who knows the policy might instead pick the moment. This chart shows the probability that barrier 1 is covered at each point of its cycle (Δ = h, W = h) under three phase rules.

        Look closer · worth discussing
        With φ ∼ U[0, Δ−W), the instant at the very start of each cycle (and the times just after it) is never covered: window j starts at φ ≥ 0 and window j−1 ended at φ − Δ + W < 0. The average is still W/Δ, so Remark 7 holds for an attacker who strikes at a random time, but an attacker who knows the cycle boundaries and times the attack beats it. Drawing φ ∼ U[0, Δ) and letting the last window wrap around to the next cycle (as the paper's own toy timetable does at midnight) makes coverage exactly W/Δ at every moment. Slide the attack time to 0 to see the difference.

        7.3 Aircraft assignment

        Collect all sorties, sort them by start time, and hand each to the next aircraft in round-robin order that is (a) free: its previous sortie started at least G = τp + τr hours ago, and (b) within budget: it has flown at most F − τp hours so far.

        Section 8

        Minimum fleet size

        Paper pp. 10–11: Definition 4, Eqs. 16–18, Remark 10

        Turn the question around: to guarantee every barrier is covered at least a fraction θ of the time, how many aircraft do you need?

        Pk ≥ θ needs hk ≥ θnkT/μ. Add over all types, require the total to fit in the budget N·F, and round up:

        \[ N_\text{min}(\theta) = \left\lceil \frac{\theta\,T\,N_b}{\mu F} \right\rceil \qquad\text{(Eq. 16)} \]
        Two thresholds that matter θ = ½ is the zero expected loss fleet (z* ≥ 0, Eq. 17): every barrier at least a coin-flip covered. θ = 1 is guaranteed full coverage (Eq. 18): every barrier protected all the time.

          Section 11

          Why the floor/ceiling mix is worst-case optimal

          Paper pp. 14–16: Definitions 5–6, Lemma 4, Theorem 5, Remarks 11–12

          Many mixtures reproduce h* on average, and they all give the same expected payoff z*. Wouldn't a wilder mix (sometimes 0 sorties, sometimes 6) be more unpredictable, and so better? The paper proves it is not: it can only hurt.

          11.1 Realised utility

          In a month where plan j is flown, an attack on type k pays U(j)(k) = vk(2μmjkτp/(nkT) − 1). A patient attacker can wait for a bad month, so judge a mix by its worst realised payoff:

          \[ W(s) = \min_{j:\,s_j>0}\ \min_k\ U_d^{(j)}(k). \]

          11.2 Lemma 4: you can't avoid the floor

          If m*k is fractional, any valid mix must include some plan with mjk ≤ ⌊m*k⌋. If every plan had at least ⌈m*k⌉, the average would be at least ⌈m*k⌉ > m*k. Contradiction.

          11.3 Theorem 5: floor/ceil hits the best possible worst case

          \[ W^* = \min_k\, v_k\!\left(\frac{2\mu\lfloor m_k^*\rfloor\tau_p}{n_k T} - 1\right), \qquad W(s) \le W^* \ \ \forall s, \qquad W(s_\text{FC}) = W^*. \]

          Upper bound: by the lemma, every mix has a month with at most the floor on each type, so W(s) ≤ W*. Achievability: floor/ceil plans never go below the floor, so they reach W*.

          Design your own mix (toy problem, m*H = 2.5)

          Choose a “low” plan and a “high” plan for High (Medium gets the other sorties out of 6). The probabilities are forced by the requirement that the average be 2.5. Widen the spread and watch the worst month.

          11.4 Why diversification cannot help

          Push one plan below the floor and another must go above the ceiling to keep the average. The high month is nice, but the patient attacker simply ignores it and strikes in the low month, where the payoff is strictly below W*.

          Remark 11. If the attacker must commit to a target before seeing which plan is active, every valid mix gives the same z*, and diversification neither helps nor hurts. The theorem is about the patient, adaptive attacker. Toggle the attacker type above to see this.

          Remark 12 (integrality gap). W* ≤ z*; the gap is the price of whole sorties. With many sorties the floors are close to m*, and W* → z*.

          Section 12

          Comparison with common-sense patrol policies

          Paper pp. 16–18, Table 5

          Is all this game theory worth it? The paper pits the Stackelberg answer against four policies a sensible planner might try, all judged the same way: the attacker hits your weakest type.

          Five policies, one attacker

          Each group shows the defender's payoff Ud(k) per type; the black tick is the game value (the lowest bar, where the attacker strikes). Click a policy to read why it fails.

          Two lessons

          1. Only Stackelberg equalises the payoffs, so the attacker has no preferred target. Every other policy leaves a weakest link.
          2. The intuitive policies are the worst randomised ones. Value-proportional and greedy pour hours into high-value targets and starve the low-value ones below 50% coverage. Plain uniform random does better than both.
