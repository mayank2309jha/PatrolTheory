Taking this idea forward

One pattern, many places

Strip away the aircraft and barriers and the paper is about something very general: how to spend limited effort when someone on the other side is watching how you behave and will aim at your weakest point. That situation turns up in exams, sport, cards, money, your commute and code review. Once you can see its outline, you start noticing it everywhere. Just as important, you'll notice where it doesn't apply.

The four ingredients The paper's machinery is worth using when all four are present:
  1. An opponent whose gain is (roughly) your loss.
  2. They learn your habits (your policy) over time…
  3. …but can't see today's move before committing to theirs.
  4. You have too little to protect everything.

Six portable lessons

Each is a result from the paper, rewritten as a habit of thought you can carry anywhere.

  1. Judge a plan by its weakest point, not its average. If someone can choose where to hit you, your plan is only as good as its worst target. (Max–min, §4)
  2. Equalise. At the best plan the opponent is indifferent: every target is equally unattractive. If one spot is clearly the weakest, shift effort there. (Remark 3)
  3. Sometimes abandon something, deliberately. When resources are very short, leaving a low-value target completely open can be the optimal choice, not a failure. (§5.3, Case 3)
  4. Randomise what they can observe. A predictable routine is a gift to anyone watching. A known rule with a hidden draw is much harder to exploit. (Stage 2, Stage 3, Fixed-patrol baseline)
  5. Keep the randomness tight. Being “wildly unpredictable” only creates bad days a patient opponent will wait for. Vary as little as needed. (Theorem 5, floor/ceiling)
  6. Know the price. Ask how many resources you need to reach a target level of safety, and what each extra unit buys. (§8, minimum fleet size)

Translate it to anything

Every concept in the paper has a counterpart in each setting below. Pick one and read down the column: the paper's vocabulary becomes a checklist for thinking about the new problem.

The concept translator

Case 1 · Preparing for a viva (exactly Stage 1)

An examiner who knows the syllabus, and soon learns how you think, will probe your weakest topic. You have a fixed number of study hours. This is not just an analogy: it is the same mathematics as Stage 1. Topics are barrier types, study hours are flying hours, and the examiner is the attacker.

  • Mastery of topic t after ht hours: Pt = ht/ct, where ct is the hours needed for full mastery (cf. Pk = μhk/nkT).
  • If asked about topic t you lose wt(1 − Pt) marks. The examiner asks where you lose most.
  • Minimise the worst loss ⇒ equalise the losses ⇒ a closed form just like Theorem 1: L* = (Σct − H) / Σ(ct/wt), and h*t = ct(1 − L*/wt). A topic with wt ≤ L* gets zero hours: Case 3 again.
Plan your study hours

Edit the topics. Marks = what's at stake if that topic is probed; hours to master = how much work full confidence takes.

These are the same four baselines as the paper's §12, and they fail for the same reasons. “Equal hours” ignores that topics differ. “Proportional to marks” over-studies the big topic. “Biggest topic first” leaves a topic untouched for the examiner to find. In real life this doesn't mean “study nothing for small topics”. It means that when time is truly short, deliberately skipping a low-stakes topic can beat spreading yourself thin.

Case 2 · A penalty kick (indifference, Remark 3)

The kicker picks a side; the goalkeeper must dive before seeing the ball. Keepers study scouting reports, so they know a kicker's tendencies but not this kick. That is all four ingredients. The kicker is the defender and the keeper is the attacker who best-responds to the known tendency.

The counter-intuitive bit: the optimal kicker goes to their stronger side less often than you'd guess. That isn't because it's worse, but because the keeper would otherwise lean that way. Just as in Stage 1, you don't maximise what you're best at; you make the opponent indifferent. Economists who studied thousands of professional penalties found that players' mixing was remarkably close to this kind of equilibrium prediction.

Case 3 · Bluffing at poker (making their choice irrelevant)

You bet; your opponent holds a hand that beats only a bluff and must decide whether to call. They know your tendencies over many hands but not this hand. If you never bluff, they always fold and you win small. If you bluff too much, they always call. The right bluffing frequency makes them indifferent between calling and folding, exactly like z* equalising the barrier types.

How often should a bet be a bluff?

The balanced frequency B/(P + 2B) depends only on the bet-to-pot ratio, much as dk depends only on the barrier's numbers. It is a policy chosen in advance; which hands you actually bluff with is the hidden daily draw.

Case 4 · Your route home (the price of unpredictability)

Choosing a route against traffic has no opponent: traffic doesn't learn your habits, so randomising buys nothing. Take the fastest route, or the one with the best worst case if lateness is costly. But if someone is watching your routine, the paper's Fixed-patrol baseline applies: a perfectly predictable route can be intercepted 100% of the time. That someone might be a stalker, a pickpocket gang, or a paparazzo; personal-safety advice to “vary your routes” is this idea. The question then becomes: how much travel time should you pay for unpredictability?

Trade travel time for unpredictability

Notice the optimal mix: fill the fastest route up to the cap, then the next fastest, and so on. Like the floor/ceiling rule, you only vary as much as you need to. A watcher waits on your most likely route, so your risk is the largest probability, not the average.

Case 5 · A portfolio (max–min against a pessimistic world)

Markets are not a single attacker choosing your worst scenario. But if you assume the world may deal you the worst of a few scenarios, choosing weights becomes Stage 1's LP: maximise z subject to z ≤ your return in each scenario. Two differences from the paper are worth noticing. Here you split money rather than randomise it, like the continuous h* before Stage 2 turns it into whole sorties. And max–min is a choice of attitude (robustness), not a fact about markets.

Where “stock picking” fits. Picking one stock against the market isn't a Stackelberg game: the market doesn't adapt to your habits. One corner of finance does match the pattern closely, though. A large, predictable trader can be exploited by others who spot the pattern and trade ahead of them. Execution algorithms therefore often vary the size and timing of their orders: Stage 3's phase randomisation, with traders instead of aircraft.

Case 6 · Chess: a useful place where the pattern breaks

During a chess game your opponent sees your move before making theirs. That breaks ingredient 3. With nothing hidden, randomising a move can't help you in theory: chess has a deterministic best strategy (even if no one can compute it). The right tool there is looking ahead (minimax search), not mixing.

But before the game, something is hidden: which opening you will choose. Strong players prepare deeply against an opponent's favourite lines. A player with one opening is the paper's Fixed-patrol baseline: fully predictable, fully prepared against. A repertoire played with some randomness is a mixed strategy, with exactly the trade-off of the route widget above. Your best line is like the fastest route; each extra line costs study time and buys unpredictability.

The general lesson: ask what can the other side see, and when? If they see your move before acting, think ahead. If they only know your habits, mix. Many real situations, chess included, contain both phases.

Deployed in the real world

The paper's reference [1] (Tambe, Security and Game Theory, 2011) describes Stackelberg security games that were actually deployed. They are the same model as the patrol paper, with different “barriers”:

System Defender → Targets What gets randomised
ARMOR LA International Airport police → road checkpoints and terminals Where checkpoints and canine patrols are placed each day
IRIS US Federal Air Marshals → flights Which flights carry a marshal
PROTECT US Coast Guard → port and ferry targets Patrol routes and timing
TRUSTS Transit police → train lines and stations Fare-inspection schedules
PAWS Wildlife rangers → park areas Anti-poaching patrol routes

Closer to everyday life, the same idea shows up wherever someone does spot checks: random tax audits, surprise inspections, random drug testing, unannounced fire drills, a manager reviewing a random sample of pull requests, a teacher's pop quiz. In each, the checker can't cover everything, the checked party knows the policy, and a predictable schedule invites gaming. The paper's “coverage probability” becomes the probability of being checked.
